# Measuring a system against SAF

*How to evaluate the L3→L4 boundary empirically. Vendor-neutral: every measurement here
can be produced by, or demanded of, any system claiming an SAF level.*

The framework's Trust Threshold (L3→L4) requires "calibrated confidence." Field experience
since the framework's first release has shown that phrasing is **necessary but incomplete**,
in a way that matters enough to make precise. This document is the precise version.

---

## 1. Calibration is not enough: the resolution requirement

Consider two systems, each 90% accurate over 1,000 incidents, each reporting a confidence
score with every verdict.

- **System A** reports 90% confidence on every case. Right on 900.
- **System B** reports 96% confidence on 750 cases (right on 720) and 72% on the
  remaining 250 (right on 180). Also right on 900.

Both systems are **perfectly calibrated**: when they say N%, they are right N% of the time.
Expected Calibration Error is exactly 0.0000 for both. Every standard calibration check —
reliability diagrams, temperature scaling residuals — passes both.

They are not equally safe to automate. Under Murphy's decomposition of the Brier score
(reliability − resolution + uncertainty):

| | ECE | Brier | reliability | **resolution** | uncertainty |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| System A | 0.0000 | 0.0900 | 0.0000 | **0.0000** | 0.0900 |
| System B | 0.0000 | 0.0792 | 0.0000 | **0.0108** | 0.0900 |

**Resolution** — the variance of the system's confidence bands around its base rate — is
the capacity to separate its own hard cases from its easy ones. System A has none: it has
exactly two operating points, act-on-everything or act-on-nothing. There is no threshold at
which it acts on its good cases and defers its bad ones, because it cannot tell them apart.
System B, at identical accuracy, can be thresholded: act above 0.95 and it takes 750 actions,
is wrong on 30, and hands 250 cases to a human *because it knew it was on thin ice* —
against 100 wrong actions for System A at full coverage.

> **SAF classification consequence:** confidence-gated execution (an L4 governance
> requirement) is only meaningful if the confidence signal has non-trivial resolution.
> A system whose confidence is well-calibrated but non-discriminating does not satisfy
> the Trust Threshold, whatever its accuracy.

## 2. The right instrument: risk–coverage curves

For a confidence threshold τ, define:

- **coverage(τ)** — the fraction of cases the system acts on (confidence ≥ τ)
- **risk(τ)** — the error rate *within* the acted-on set

Sweeping τ traces the system's **risk–coverage curve**. Report, instead of accuracy:

1. **Risk at fixed coverage** — "at 70% automation, what fraction of actions are wrong?"
2. **Coverage at fixed risk** — "inside a 2% error budget, how much can it automate?"
   This is the quantity an autonomous action rate claim (the SAF L3/L4 metric) actually
   rests on.
3. **AURC** (area under the risk–coverage curve) for tracking a system against itself.

A change that raises accuracy while flattening this curve has made the system *less*
automatable. Accuracy alone reports it as progress.

## 3. Thresholds are per action class, not per system

A single global confidence threshold silently prices all errors equally. They are not.
With L the cost of a wrong action and B the benefit of a right one, acting is justified
when confidence exceeds

    τ*(action class) = L / (L + B)

- An action whose wrong-case cost is 20× its benefit requires confidence > 0.952.
- One where the ratio is 2× requires only > 0.667.
- **Reversibility belongs in L**: a tested undo path divides the loss, and is the only
  term in the equation engineering fully controls. This is why undo-before-autonomy is
  a governance requirement, not a convenience.
- **Severity scales L**: at the highest severity bands the required confidence exceeds
  anything a system can honestly claim, which is the formal justification for
  always-ask rules at any autonomy level.

A system offering one autonomy slider for the whole product has not done this analysis.

## 4. Where the confidence number must come from

Confidence signals that do **not** support the measurements above:

- **Verbalised self-report** ("I am 95% confident") — generated by the same process as
  the rest of the output; clusters on round numbers; carries training-set priors, not
  frequency information.
- **Sequence likelihood** — fluent wrong answers score well.
- **Confidence over missing evidence** — a system that never received a piece of
  evidence will be confidently wrong about it unless the *absence* is explicitly
  represented in its context.

Signals that do: agreement across independent evidence sources; survival of an
adversarial refutation pass; match against patterns with confirmed prior outcomes.
Confidence is a claim the system makes, not a measurement it reports — grade it like
any other output.

## 5. Evaluation-harness discipline

Two failure modes recur when teams measure any of the above, and both produce numbers
that look like data:

- **Absolute-scale judges drift.** A grader asked to score 1–10 can converge to flagging
  nearly everything, at which point it measures the scale, not the system. Prefer
  **pairwise comparison** (two candidate responses, judge picks a winner) with judge
  **position randomised**, ties allowed, and "both unacceptable" recorded as its own
  outcome. Detecting a 55% win rate against a 50% null (α = 0.05, power 0.80) requires
  **~783 decided comparisons** — results declared on a few dozen resolve nothing.
- **The instrument fails like the system.** Before concluding a metric reflects the
  system, compute what a *correct* system would score on it (an unreachable target is a
  bug in the metric), and verify the harness can distinguish "the response was bad"
  from "I could not read the response."

## 6. Summary: what to demand of an SAF level claim

| Claim | Demand |
|---|---|
| "X% accurate" | The **risk–coverage curve**, not the point estimate |
| "Calibrated confidence" | Calibration **and resolution** (Murphy decomposition or selective-risk table) |
| "Confidence-gated execution" | **τ\* per action class** and how the loss ratios were estimated |
| "Autonomous action rate of Y%" | Coverage **at a stated risk budget**, and what happens to the remainder |
| Any of the above | Which components produce these numbers in enforcement today, versus observation/shadow |

A system that cannot produce these artefacts may still be a good system.
It cannot yet be a *classified* one.
